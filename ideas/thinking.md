So the practical full-stack pipeline should be thought of in this order.

## 1. Start with the probabilistic story

Before architecture, decide what factorization you are claiming.

For a binary disease case, a realistic version is:
[
z=(z_s, z_d, z_n)
]
where (z_s) is shared anatomy/acquisition, (z_d) is disease-specific, and (z_n) is nuisance. Then decide whether you want:

* **full independence:** (p(z)=p(z_s)p(z_d)p(z_n))
* **group-wise independence:** (z_d \perp z_s) but not necessarily independent within each block
* **conditional independence:** (z_d \perp z_s \mid c) for view, sex, age, scanner, etc.

For CXR this third option is often more believable. If you skip this step, the architecture will be solving an ill-posed target. ([OpenReview][4])

## 2. Decide what kind of supervision you can afford

This is usually the real determinant of whether separation works.

If you only have image-level labels, then you can push one latent block to predict cardiomegaly and another block not to. If you have grouped data, such as same patient or same anatomy but different findings, ML-VAE style grouping becomes much stronger because it tells the model what should be shared and what should vary. If you have pairs where only one factor changes, weak supervision becomes even more powerful. If you have masks or boxes, you can align disease factors with spatial support. The literature is quite consistent that weak supervision or grouping makes disentanglement far more reliable than pure unsupervised training. ([arXiv][7])

## 3. Choose the latent structure before the encoder details

For your use case, a good progression is:

**Single latent vector** → easiest baseline, hardest to control.
**Partitioned latent vector** ((z_s, z_d)) → most useful starting point.
**Multi-head / multi-branch encoder** → useful when you want each branch to specialize.
**Hierarchical latent** → useful when you want coarse anatomy at high level and disease details at lower level.
**Spatial latent map** rather than only a vector → often better for localized pathology and later diffusion conditioning.

The main pattern in the literature is not “use CNN X to discover concept Y,” but rather “use a latent parameterization that matches the factorization you believe exists.” ([arXiv][7])

## 4. Then choose architecture as an inductive bias

For images, CNNs are still a sensible default because they encode locality and translation structure. Self-attention helps when the factor depends on long-range relations, which is relevant for cardiomegaly because the cardiac silhouette is a global shape cue relative to thoracic context. Spatial attention modules or CBAM can help the encoder focus, but by themselves they do not guarantee disentanglement. Cross-attention becomes especially useful later, when a latent diffusion model conditions on labels, masks, prompts, or latent factor tokens. It is more a **routing mechanism for conditioning** than an independence mechanism.

So a reasonable statement is:

* CNN / UNet / ResNet gives local visual feature extraction.
* Self-attention helps represent global dependencies and long-range anatomical context.
* Spatial attention or masks can help localize pathology.
* Separate heads or latent blocks support factor allocation.
* Cross-attention is mainly for injecting factor-specific conditions into the generator.

That is a much safer formulation than “design the CNN so it groups nose, mouth, face, then disentangles accessories.” The grouping may emerge, but only if the objective and data support it. ([arXiv][3])

## 5. Losses are where most of the separation actually happens

This is the main toolkit.

A base VAE gives
[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}
========================

## \mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]

\beta, D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z)).
]

Then you add structure:

* **β-VAE / KL pressure**: pushes compression and can improve disentanglement, but often harms reconstruction if too strong. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1])
* **FactorVAE / β-TCVAE**: penalize total correlation so latent dimensions are closer to independent. Better aligned with disentanglement than just increasing β. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][2])
* **DIP-VAE**: pushes aggregated posterior covariance toward diagonal, encouraging decorrelated latents with better reconstruction tradeoff. ([OpenReview][8])
* **Supervised classification loss on disease block**: forces (z_d) to be predictive of cardiomegaly.
* **Adversarial or orthogonality loss**: discourages (z_s) from predicting disease and discourages overlap between blocks.
* **Mutual information terms**: encourage a factor to remain informative about a target or invariant to another target.
* **Cross-reconstruction / swap losses**: swap (z_d) between samples while keeping (z_s), then reconstruct; this is often very useful for testing whether the model has truly isolated a factor.
* **Consistency losses with masks / boxes / attention**: if you have localization cues, align disease-specific edits to the correct region.

The important correction here is that **orthogonality is not the same as statistical independence**. It is a useful engineering surrogate, especially for linear probes and block separation, but it is weaker than independence. You can use it, just do not over-interpret it. ([OpenReview][8])

## 6. How to weight the losses

There is no universal formula, but there is a practical pattern.

Start from a reconstruction-credible baseline first. In medical images especially, if the decoder is weak, every disentanglement result becomes suspect. Then gradually increase the factorization pressure. In practice:

* If reconstruction collapses, your independence regularizer is too strong or your decoder is too weak.
* If disease leaks everywhere, your supervised or invariance losses are too weak.
* If the disease block becomes predictive but edits are unrealistic, the latent is discriminative but not generative.
* If swapping the disease block barely changes pathology, the model did not allocate enough disease information there.
* If swapping the disease block changes anatomy too, your blocks are entangled.

This is why many successful pipelines alternate between **representation quality checks** and **factor isolation checks**, instead of just optimizing a single disentanglement score. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1])

## 7. Where diffusion enters

Once you have a latent space with at least partial separability, diffusion gives you two options.

First, train a **conditional latent diffusion model** on the whole latent or image, conditioned on factor labels, masks, or factor embeddings. This is the easier path for generation and editing.

Second, train **separate conditional or factor-specific score models** whose composition at inference relies on independence assumptions. This is where your concern becomes central. Score addition is only meaningful when the component scores correspond to compatible factorized distributions. If each score model still contains mixed information, composition will produce bleeding and artifacts. That is exactly the failure mode you described. ([OpenReview][5])

So the bridge from VAE to composable diffusion is:

1. learn a latent space where factors are at least partially isolated,
2. verify that factor edits are localized and semantically clean,
3. only then attempt score-based composition.

Trying score composition before checking factor purity usually makes debugging impossible.

## 8. What supervision is most useful

For your application, the ranking is roughly:

Image-level disease labels are the minimum.
Pairs or grouped observations are much better.
Segmentation masks or boxes are strongest when the pathology is localized.
Attention maps can help as soft supervision, but they are weaker and noisier.
Pure unsupervised disentanglement is the least reliable. ([arXiv][9])

For cardiomegaly specifically, masks are not always straightforward, because the concept is partly about heart size relative to thoracic anatomy, not just a small lesion region. So anatomical priors or shape-aware supervision may matter more than for small focal diseases.

## 9. A realistic experiment ladder

A clean end-to-end experimental ladder for you would be:

Start with a strong VAE baseline that reconstructs CXR well.
Partition latent into shared and disease blocks.
Add disease supervision only to the disease block.
Add invariance or adversarial pressure so shared block cannot predict disease.
Add TC/DIP-style regularization gently.
Test with linear probes on each block.
Test latent swapping: anatomy fixed, disease edited.
Test counterfactual reconstructions and whether edits are anatomically plausible.
Then train latent diffusion with factor conditioning.
Only after that, try score addition or probabilistic composition.

That gives you a debugging chain from “representation exists” to “representation is editable” to “representation supports composition.”

## 10. The most important conceptual takeaway

The clean ordered thinking process is:

**assumption → latent factorization → supervision → objective → architecture → generator/composition test**

not

**architecture → hope that independence emerges.**

So yes, architecture matters a lot, but mostly as a **bias for how information is routed and represented**. Independence assumptions should come from your scientific model of the data. Then you choose a VAE latent structure and losses that operationalize those assumptions. Then diffusion is layered on top once you have evidence that the factors are genuinely isolatable.

For your specific concern about “normal” and “cardiomegaly,” I would not recommend claiming full independence. I would recommend claiming a weaker and more honest target:

[
x \sim p(x \mid z_{\text{shared}}, z_{\text{cardio}}, z_{\text{nuisance}})
]
with the goal that (z_{\text{cardio}}) is **maximally predictive and editable for cardiomegaly**, while (z_{\text{shared}}) retains anatomy and acquisition factors and is **as invariant as possible** to cardiomegaly. That is statistically more defensible and experimentally easier to validate.
